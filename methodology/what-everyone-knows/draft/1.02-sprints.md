## 1.02 - Sprints

Have you run a marathon? I think it's one of those things you don't need to try yourself to understand that it's very difficult and demanding. Most people can't do it physically (me, for example). To finish a marathon you must be in good shape, have good health, and be properly trained. But if we take a look at your entire running history, and sum up the segments you ever run, what do we get? How many marathons have we run in our lives? Even without special preparations and training, every month we cover huge distances just by moving in short segments every day. It's a very simple logic and it works in many areas of your life. And that's **What Everyone Knows**. 

Of course it's not the same as a real marathon. But from the perspective of the work accomplished, it's the same amount of work done over time. And sometimes even more, because you start every new chunk full of energy and motivation.

In engineering, we often try to run a marathon at sprint speed. This leads to **Thermal Throttling**  (burnout). I saw many good engineers who came to the company and started to work at a light speed, but one or maybe two years later I've seen their lifeless eyes. It's not because they were not properly trained or had a bad shape, but just because they were drowning in the chaos of unresolved issues and technical debt. Even top-trained and strongest athletes can't handle a train cart falling on their heads. The only way I see is to learn how to avoid such consequences.  

In this chapter, I want to talk about sub-tasking, how to use it, and when and why it's needed. I argue that sub-tasking isn't just "organization" - it's **Flow Management** which helps you to not lose control and not dip into the chaos working on big complex tasks. And below I'll try to explain why.
 
### Example 1

Imagine a common task: you need to upgrade the “Service A” from version `2.x` to `3.x`. On the surface, it’s a standard ticket that might simply read:  

` • Upgrade Service A to 3.x across all environments.`  

But let's look closer. This is a major update. In our world, that likely means these versions are incompatible and require data or metadata migration. Once that migration is finished, there may be no way to roll back. What does this imply? It means that we should be well-prepared; blindly updating production is a recipe for disaster. Suddenly, a “trivial” task becomes complex and potentially dangerous. Now, we need to design rollout and rollback plans, test the 
upgrade path, update staging, hit production, and then review all related documentation - the list goes on.  
So, you start your task. You're discovering what you need to do during the upgrade, and writing a plan. In the middle of the work you remember that you must check metrics to ensure that all you need exists. You switch to the metrics and get stuck there for a while. Then you return back, read the plan draft from the beginning to restore the context, but get distracted by Ansible, because you must check if your Ansible-collection supports a config option you must update 
during the upgrade. And so, you are in a wild whirlwind.

This is exactly how I approached things in the beginning. I perceived such tasks as monoliths. While working on one part, I would stumble upon new questions that needed solving. I’d switch to those new questions, falling into a chaotic, uncontrollable whirlwind. Of course, I produced some results every day, but by the evening, I felt a deep sense of frustration. I couldn’t measure my progress. I had a persistent feeling that the task was growing faster than I could resolve it, because I was uncovering more questions that I was providing answers to.

So, let’s figure out exactly why this trap works, and why we fell into it.  
 
---

### The Anatomy of Monolith Task.
First things first. To fix a problem we must understand its symptoms. Let's go in order, and take a quick look at each obstacle we might encounter while working on a monolith task.

**Context Switching Cost.**  
First, monoliths - or "elephants", as it is called in time management - cause more context switches. We already spoke about the harmful influence of context switches on our productivity in the previous chapter (and will more in the future), so I will not dwell on this for long.  
Big tasks have large and complex context models. This requires more time to load and unload them from working memory. Also, if you are working on a big task the whole day, then any interruption, any incoming message is a distraction for you which forces context switching. Every time, your brain must remember where it stopped, reload the context and so on. At the same time, such big context models have huge Attention Residue, slowing down the performance of every task that interrupts you. It takes a lot of your time.

**The Result Measurement.**  
It's a very underrated symptom, but on a long distance it becomes crucial. When you work on a monolith task, it’s hard to understand how far you have come during the day, because you are permanently switching from one thought to another (even inside one task). Working on configuration you see a property and remember that you didn’t describe it in the documentation. Then, you switch to documentation, write down the part you just remembered, and return to the configuration. As a result, at the very end of the day, you have many small outcomes from different parts of the task. This result is very difficult to measure.
If you can’t see a clear result for a while, it can become a demotivating factor. This leads to a chronic dissatisfaction with your work. This dissatisfaction lowers motivation, reduces productivity, and eventually leads to burnout. I presume many of us are familiar with this feeling: you’ve worked hard the whole day, you are exhausted, yet at the very end of the day, you don’t see a significant outcome. You feel like nothing was actually done.  

It’s a horrible feeling. I know it well.

**Monolith Paralysis.**  
That's a classic psychological hurdle. When we face a monolith task, our brain doesn't see a career opportunity or a creative project; it sees a predatory beast. Have you noticed that sometimes you don't want to start a task because it feels complex and obscure? It usually comes down to three psychological triggers.   

First, we deal with the **"Amygdala Hijack"**. A massive, ill-defined task triggers the  brain's threat center (the amygdala). Since the task feels impossible to complete in one sitting, the brain perceives it as a source of certain failure or pain, triggering a "freeze" response. But in this state, the brain doesn't just "freeze", it actively seeks "immediate gratification" to offset the stress. This is why you suddenly find yourself cleaning your desk, checking email, or scrolling a social network. Your brain is trading the Long-Term Value of the project for a Short-Term Dopamine Hit to escape the "threat" of the task.  
Then comes **"Decision Fatigue"**, also known as the "Where do I start?" problem. A monolith task often lacks a clear entry point. The mental energy required just to figure out the first step is often higher than the energy available, so we opt for a low-energy activity instead (like scrolling social media).  
Finally, we hit the wall of **Low Self-Efficacy**. According to psychologist Albert Bandura's theory, if we don't believe we can successfully execute a task, we won't try. A monolith feels "un-executable", crushing our confidence before we even pick up a pen.

**The "What is next?" Tax.**  
When a task is not decomposed into strict, granular chunks, you pay a "tax" every few minutes.
You finish writing a function. You stop. You look at the 500 lines of code remaining. Your brain asks: *"What should I do next?"*. And here we are again, the "Decision Fatigue" face to face.
This question is a **vulnerability**. It's a gap in your "system's defenses" where procrastination enters. Because your brain is tired of the big task, it chooses the easiest "next step": *Check Slack, open a news tab, or get another coffee*.

**The Fog of War.**
Since you are discovering the map on the executing phase without a proper plan, you're opening the landscape in real time, and can only see the next 5 meters. You don't know if there is a landmine (such as circular dependency or a breaking change) 20 meters ahead.You move slowly and tentatively because you are afraid of what you can't see.

**The Fog of Recall.**  
Probably the most dangerous symptom. Because it not only slows your progress, but in some circumstances can be destructive. Our memory is not perfect. Trying to hold such a large and complex object in your head is very risky. You can just forget or miss something important. In a positive scenario - you just lose some time, but sometimes it can cost hours of uptime of the service you're responsible for.  

Searching we can find many more symptoms which block or slow your productivity, but most of them upon deep analysis most of them will be reduced to the six I listed above.

### The Science of the "Chunk".
To explain my point more properly I want to go deep into a theory. The first thing I want to draw your attention to is the human's Reward System. Returning to the marathon-sprint analogy, it's not necessary to finish a marathon to force your brain to release another micro-dose of dopamine. It's doing that at every "milestone" and every accomplished task. So, every time when you tick another checkbox in your To-Do list, you are getting another micro-dose of chemical reward. That feeling of satisfaction isn't just a mental quirk - it's a biological reward system in action.  
Why is it so important?  

To understand why dopamine is so vital for productivity, we have to look past its reputation as a "pleasure chemical" and see it for what it truly is: **the molecule of inspiration and anticipation**.

#### The Engine of Effort.
Dopamine is the brain's way of signaling that something is worth doing. Evolutionary, it helped our ancestors remember where the fruit trees were and gave them a drive to return to them. Today, it serves as the bridge between intention and action. Without a healthy dopamine response, tasks feel heavy, grey, and insurmountable. By breaking a massive project into tiny "chunks", you aren't just organizing your time, you are creating a series of finish lines. This does three critical things: Positive Reinforcement, Energy Boost, and Stress Reduction.  
The **Positive Reinforcement** validates the effort you just spent, making you likely to repeat behavior. You happily move on to the next task on your list. Because you feel you can accomplish it, just like you did last time. Then dopamine give us **Energy Boost**. That provides a temporary lift in focus and physical energy, which you can then pivot into the text task. And finally the **Stress Reduction**. Completing a task reduces the cognitive load of an unfinished "open loop", lowering cortisol levels and making you feel more in control.

If you only aim for one massive goal (e.g., "Write a Book"), your brain perceives the reward as being too far away. The "reward-to-effort" ration looks lopsided, and you are likely to procrastinate because the dopamine payoff is months down the line. By splitting that goal into "Write 200 words", on the other hand, you shorten the feedback loop. This creates something like a "success spiral" - where dopamine from "Task A" gives you the momentum to start "Task B", turning a daunting mountain into a series of manageable steps.

#### Cognitive Closure (The 'Open Loops' Problem)
I can't help but remember the Zeigarnik Effect we spoke about in the previous chapter (like the context switching, it is destined to follow us throughout the book). As we already know, the human brain remembers unaccomplished tasks better than completed ones. This unaccomplished task creates "Open Loops" that drain background energy. In case of monoliths this "Open Loop" stays open for days and weeks.  
Evolutionarily, this was a survival mechanism: if you were interrupted while hunting or gathering, your brain needed to "itch" until you finished the job. In the modern world, however, a massive project that takes three weeks to finish acts as one giant, persistent Open Loop. Because the loop remains open, your brain refuses to "flush the cache", keeping every stressful detail in your active working memory. This creates a state of *Persistent Interrupt Latency*. Your nervous system signals a "System Alert" because the process hasn't exited with a 0 code. You are stuck in a `Running` state for weeks, consuming CPU cycles on "Background Anxiety" instead of "productive Execution".

To use a technical analogy, a monolithic task is a *Kafka partition with an infinite segment size*. Because the segment never "rolls", the broker can't archive old data, and the index files become bloated and slow to search. Mentally, you are a Consumer that refuses to *commit offsets* until the end of the month. If you "rebalance" (get a phone call or go to sleep), you have to re-process the entire stream from the beginning because you never saved your progress. Small chunks are your *Offset Commits*. They allow you to "checkpoint" your mental state, so you can start the next session exactly where you left off, with a clean buffer.

By breaking a project into atomic units, you are effectively forcing a segment roll. Every time you finish a small chunk, you achieve cognitive closure. This triggers Mental Garbage Collection, allowing the brain to discard the "how-to" noise and intermediate stress of that specific step. You "flush the cache", keeping only the "final value" of the work done and freeing up 100% of your working memory for the next offset. In short: to keep your internal broker running at peak performance, you have to roll your segments often.

#### The "7±2" Rule.
To complete our architectural map of the mind, we must look at the physical constraints of our "hardware" through the lens of **George Miller**. In 1956, Miller, a cognitive psychologist, published one of the most cited papers in psychology, establishing what we now call **Miller's Law**.

He discovered that the human mind has a remarkably tight bottleneck: our working memory can only hold approximately $7±2$ objects or "chunks" of information at any given time. This isn't just a suggestion; it is a hard physical limit on our cognitive RAM.

When you tackle a *monolith*, you are essentially trying to bypass Miller's Law. A large, complex project often requires you to balance 20 to 30 different variables, dependencies, and constraints simultaneously. You are thinking about the database schema, the API endpoint, the CSS styling, and the deadline all at once.  
In technical terms, this creates a **Buffer Overflow**. When the number of active variables exceeds the $7±2$ limit, the brain doesn't just "slow down" - it starts dropping data. This is where "stupid" mistakes, logic bugs, and forgotten edge cases come from. You lose the "pointer" to a critical piece of information because your mental stack is full.

**Reducing the "Active Set".**  
Breaking a monolith into chunks is a strategy for **Context Encapsulation**. By isolating a specific 4-hour task, you define a boundary. Within that boundary, you only need to hold maybe 3 or 4 variables in your head to succeed.  
This significantly reduces your **Active Set** - the collection of data your processor needs immediate access to. By keeping the Active Set well below the miller Limit, you ensure that you don't have to consistently "swap" information in and out of your long-term memory, and since your buffer isn't overflowing, you can maintain 100% integrity on the variables you are handling.  

#### The Planning Fallacy: The "Optimistic Compiler"  
To complete our architectural audit of productivity, we have to address the *runtime estimation error*. Even if we manage our dopamine and respect Miller's Law, we still run into the "Planning Fallacy" - the cognitive bias that makes us chronically optimistic about how long a project will take.  
The **Planning Fallacy**, proposed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, suggests that humans naturally underestimate the time, costs, and risks of future actions while overestimating the benefits. When we look at a monolith, our brain performs a happy path compilation. We see the start and the end, but we ignore the **Unknown Unknowns** - the merge conflicts, the broken dependencies, and the sudden meetings that haunt every large project.

The larger the task, the more "surface area" there is for these hidden variables to strike. Because a monolith is a single, opaque block, we can't see the complexity hidden inside it, leading to a massive discrepancy between our "Estimated Time" and "Actual Runtime".

This is where we lean on the **Law of Large Numbers**. In probability theory, this law states that as the number of trials increases, the average of the results becomes closer to the expected value.  
When you work on a single, massive 40-hour task, you have exactly one data point for your estimate. If you hit a snag (an "unknown unknown"), your entire timeline is blown by 50% or 100%. There is no room for error correction.  
However, when you break that monolith into twenty 2-hour chunks, this increases the estimation accuracy. It is physically easier for the brain to visualize a 2-hour window. You can see the "edges" of the task. Second point is **Error Cancellation** - you might underestimate Chunk A by 15 minutes, but you will likely overestimate Chunk B by 10 minutes. Over a long enough "sprint" of small tasks, these minor errors tend to *cancel each other out*.  

---

#### Drawing the line.
When we combine these concepts, the picture becomes clear. **Dopamine** provides the fuel to start; the **Zeigarnik Effect** prevents background energy leaks by closing segments; and **Miller's Law** dictates the maximum size of the segment we can safely process.  

Working in small chunks is essentially **Object-Oriented Productivity**. You are taking a massive, messy "Spaghetti Code" project and refactoring it into clean, encapsulated functions. Each function (chunk) has its own local variables, its own clear output, and - most importantly - it fits perfectly within the limited cache of the human CPU.  

By modularizing your work this way, you also bypass the **Planning Fallacy**. Instead of the giant, inaccurate estimate from a monolith, you create a series of small, manageable "trials". This allows the **Law of Large Numbers** to work in your favor: the minor timing errors of each individual chunk tend to cancel each other out over the course of the day. You move from a "Stochastic" workflow, where one "unknown unknown" can derail your entire week, to a **Deterministic Timeline** - where your output is as predictable and reliable as a well-compiled program.

### The Atomic Task: A Practical Framework.  
If you have read this far you already know how our brain perceives a complex monolith tasks, and why it's better to split them into small chunks. However, it's not enough to start using this approach in our daily life. It requires more than just a to-do list - it requires **Atomic Tasks**. An atomic task is the smallest unit of work that still retains its integrity and provides a clear transition point for the brain. To identify these, we must use some rules (or filters) which afford us what we still can split and what not.  

Let's start from the simplest and more obvious criteria - an estimated duration. In an ideal world, my favorite duration for an atomic task is 3 hours. Why three? I use pretty simple logic: I can fit two 3-hour task into one 8-hour days, and also there is another 2 hour which can be used for meetings, and other ad-hoc activities, such as code-reviews, alerts, and questions from colleagues. However, since some tasks can be shorter, up to 1-2h , I propose to use **The 4-Hour Rule**. Let's formalize it as:

> Rule 1: If a task takes more than 4 hours, it's still a monolith. Split it.

So, we split monolith in such a way that its parts' estimated duration do not exceed 4-hour interval. The chunk can be shorter if the task takes less time, but cannot be larger. This rule still affords us to fit us two to three task into 8-hour day (e.g., $4+4$, $4+3$, $4+2+1$, etc.), depending on your schedule and your availability on a particular day.  
Why The 4-Hour Rule works? On one side of the scale, four hours represents the natural limit of "Deep Work" before cognitive fatigue sets in. After four hours of concentrated work you are getting tired, and your brain needs for a brake. Here you can take a lunch brake, relax, chat with colleagues, and give your brain time to rest. On the other side, by capping a task at four hours, you ensure that you can achieve **Cognitive Closure** at least twice a day. This forces a "segment roll" before your mental buffer becomes too bloated with intermediate variables. If you can't see the finish line within a four-hour window, you haven't decomposed the problem deeply enough.
