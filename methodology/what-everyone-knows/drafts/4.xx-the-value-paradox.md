# The Value Paradox: Engineering Integrity vs. Budget Cynicism [DRAFT]

I once had a CTO tell me that the engineering department is "just a cost". The logic was that engineers don't generate revenue like sales or account management do. Back then, I temporary lost my motivation for couple days. *"Can an engineer value the CTO who does not value his engineers?"* I thought.
I can't agree with his point of view. And it was this disagreement that led to the chapter you are currently reading.

## The "Cost Center" Fallacy

The logic inside this kind of managers is pretty simple: developers has written a code, SRE prepared infrastructure, now all you need is just deploy the code into prepared infrastructure and it will make money itself. 
But this is the line of reasoning of someone who isn't technically inclined and doesn't understand how the system actually works.
It is our job to convey the true state of affairs to the managers.

If we put ourselves in their shoes, we can understand their point of view. All they see is a "Black Box". On one side they see money (salaries) flowing into this "black box", on other side they see new features emerging from it.

But they do not see the maintenance, the entropy, or the risk mitigation. When you automate another "toil" process, only your team sees that you saved another 200 man-hours per year (and as we know $Time=Money$). But from a business perspective, the company just spent another money for the system maintenance. It's especially related to SRE—if everything is working perfectly, it looks like you are doing nothing. This is **SRE Paradox**.  

The only point when an engineer's work becomes visible is the time when something doesn't work. This creates very dangerous connection between *Engineers* and *losing of money* inside top-level managers' minds.  

We will talk down below a little more regarding the claim that engineers don't bring in money. But before we move on, I want you to understand the main idea, which is very simple. Engineers build the engine that drives the business. Without that engine, even a Ferrari is just a very expensive, stationary sofa. It might look good in the showroom, but it’s not going anywhere.  

> *Coders, SREs, architects, ITSecs are partners who understand how technology enables business, and how bits become a real money.* 

## The Math of Invisible Value
To provide a mathematical basis for what I'm talking about, let's run a simple mental health check: *"What is the revenue of your top-tier sales team when system uptime is zero?"*. Exactly. It’s zero. These two values are directly dependent on. **Revenue is not a constant; it is a function of uptime $(U)$ and performance $(P)$.**

$$Revenue=∫(Sales⋅Performance)dt⋅Uptime$$

If $Uptime=0$, then $Revenue=0$.

Ultimately, the best service generates no revenue if no one sells it, and the best sales team is powerless if the service is slow, unreliable, or insecure. So, if insufficient effort and resources are devoted to areas such as maintenance, disaster recovery, optimization, and so on, the entire system—including revenue—begins to suffer. In our highly competitive digital world a user can easily switch to your competitor's service if he notices any minor annoying issue (e.g, being logged out at a crucial moment for him/her) in your one.

Engineering value is often measured in what *didn't* happen (outages, data leaks, slow-downs). Business struggles to value "The Silence", but as SREs, **Silence is our greatest product**.  
We are doing everything in our power to keep the system in a state where, from the perspective of users and businesses, it appears as though nothing is happening—even though, in reality, that is not always the case. All these Replication Factors, Disaster Recovery clusters, graceful switching users between nodes during a maintenance, etc. serve only one purpose—to enable business to make more money by ensuring uninterrupted uptime.  

I understand the struggle: it is incredibly difficult to measure the value of something not happening. You cannot use the same gauge for Engineering as you do for Sales. When you manufacture tires, you simply count the units produced. But if you start measuring an SRE’s performance by the number of incidents fixed—I have bad news for you—you’ve hit a dead end. This is why most KPI systems fail in the world of SRE and other professions where the primary product is The Silence. But just because it’s hard to quantify doesn’t mean the effort isn't valuable.
