# The Hardware: Distraction-Free Environment
Talking about deep work, one more thing I'd like to point out is the productive environment, your work place and things that surround you. *You'll never be able to concentrate if your workspace is in the way of your family members' path to the fridge.* And this is **What Everyone Knows.** 

Bellow in this *(sub-)* chapter I'll give you few **recommendations** which can be easily integrated into your workflow and work environment. But these are not a strict rules (like all I tell about in this project), these are just a hints and tricks which can help you to reduce the environmental pressure to your brain, and increase your concentration ability, to *work less but do more*.
Some of these recommendations can be applicable only to your home workplace, others - only to an office. Don't worry if you can't apply all of them, this is absolutely normal. But remember, the more (of them) you can apply, the greater (their) positive impact will be.  

First, talking about the surroundings, I’d like to talk about your work desk and its placement in the room. After yet another move, while arranging the furniture in a new apartment, I made a very serious mistake that I soon regretted. I didn't have a separate office, so I set up my desk in the living room in such a way that, while sitting at it, I could see the entire room. So, working at the desk I saw all that was going on in the living room. Before I did it, I didn't assume how distractive and destructive this decision would be, even if there is no one in the room except yourself. When your eyes see the entire room, your brain latches onto every detail and each thing that's out of place. The cup you have forgotten to remove from the coffee table, the houseplant that you mustn't forget to water, the dust you noticed on the picture frame, and so on. My brain was overloaded with all this information which it continuously got from the environment. When there is someone in the room, the number of distractions multiplies, because your restless brain is trying to control all movements and changes in the room which it can see from its point of view.  

Once I turned my desk so that I was facing the wall, the situation improved. It became much easier to focus because the visual noise got to a minimum. So, let's formulate the first recommendation:  

> The less you see around you, sitting at the desk, the less distractions you get from the environment.  

Sounds like something very obvious, but I see this mistake very often in people's homes and offices. (This is one of these recommendations which may be beyond your control sometimes, in office spaces, for example.)  
The sitting that way that you see the door can be useful for people who work with clients, but for "knowledge-workers" (I classify engineers specifically into this category) that can be very distractive, and can lower your productivity to almost zero.  

Another point the first recommendation can be applied to is "stuff" at your desk. Very often people have many small things at their desks: sticking notes, photos, Funko Pop collections, and many other things that are dear to them. I understand why people collect these items on their desks, but from a productiveness' point of view, all these items are subject to the same rule: "the more items before your eyes - the more distractions your brain gets". Every time you look at your partner photo your brain switches to thoughts about your partner, every time when you see your Funky Pop collection you think which one you must buy next. Every eye movement creates another context switch, and your brain just can't ignore these interruptions, because for it this is just another batch of information it got through the input device (eyes) and it has to process this data.

> The number of objects you see while working is directly proportional to the number of distractions your brain receives. 

Even your paper notebook, if it's open on the desk before you, it is another distraction for your brain. The brain immediately latches onto every unfinished task that happens to catch your eye. If you want to help your brain to clean the cache from unnecessary for its work information, just remove all that may distract you so you don't see it while you are working. Close your notebook if you don't need it for the current task, or if the current page contents something but current task related information. Following this rule you remove the extra load on your "CPU" reducing context switches for background processes and relieving extra throughput for the current task.

The same approach is related to software as well. My worst decision was when I tried to keep a corporate instant messenger (e.g., Slack) always open before my eyes on the second display. I wanted to always be in-sync and miss nothing. But in fact it takes a huge part of my attention giving nothing back. You don't need the most messages you see, they are just a noise in your peripheral vision. If you are worried to miss something important you can enable notification for direct messages and mentions. You will never miss if someone writes to you directly or tags you, but at the same time you will remove redundant and unnecessary notifications. Once your "Deep Work" session is over, you can open Slack and read carefully all what was happening while you worked. This is not only about productivity itself, it's just "digital hygiene".  

Discovering the "right" setup  for my work laptop I tried many different approaches and different configurations. It took me more than a year to identify the main requirements for my workspace and to understand what specifically allows me to concentrate more on my work and get distracted less. The main goal of that small research was to spend time only once and in result get strict universal rules I can use the rest of my life. Many of the requirements I had gotten when the "research" was finished were pretty obvious, but that was a proof for my restless mind which explained why I must use what I use.  

First, and probably the most important is Local System Stability. Using different Operating Systems (e.g, Slackware, Arch, Fedora) on my laptops during the first five years of my career I noticed that some weeks I spend up to 4-5 hours just for the system maintenance. Something happens from time to time, broken bluetooth after another system update, problems with external displays and HiDPI, sleep state and power consumption, screen sharing and camera, and so on. Every time I found myself fixing my laptop yet again, I felt that something wasn't quite right. On the one hand, I enjoyed doing that; on the other, I realized I was wasting my time. What conclusions can we draw from this short story? If you value your time:

> Choose tools which require minimal maintenance.

As of this writing, I use two main operating systems: macOS for work and Fedora Workstation for personal projects. These OSs afford me not to think about the small and insignificant details and to focus on what really matters, because all the basic features that the average user doesn't need to worry about, such as screen sharing, bluetooth, sleep mode, etc., just work out of the box. I’m not saying you have to use the same ones, but I do argue that you should choose a tool that allows you to spend less time on its maintenance, by providing the necessary configuration.  

We have already spoken about how important it is to focus on one task at a time to reach a better result. That approach works just as well for our digital workspace. When you have several different applications before your eyes, it's distractive as well as open loops your brain tries to process in a background. The solution lay on the surface.

> Only one app must be open on screen at time.

Of course you mustn't suffer when your current task requires few applications at the same time. This recommendation is just a recommendation, and can have exceptions. If the task requires few apps, and these apps are parts of the same flow, you can see them together. To clarify what "parts of the same flow" means, let's look at some examples. (1) I'm rolling out changes to production working on the main display and at the same time I have graphs (related to the service I'm working on) opened on the second screen. It can't be very convenient when you must see the results of your work in real time, to reduce the response time in case something goes wrong. (2) I'm preparing a plan for a maneuver, and have a browser with opened documentation, and my notes in the split-screen. In this case these two apps also are parts of the same flow. I'm writing a plan and checking the documentation at the same time to make sure I don't miss anything.  
We can find many examples like these when few open applications really matter, but if it's possible to reduce the number of applications before your eyes, it should be reduced.  

There are many tools that can help you to implement this approach into your workflow. Those can be Aerospace or Yabai for macOS, or maybe i3 or Sway for Linux; it's not so important which tools you use. What is important - which result you get. Focused work is like driving through a tunnel, when you see only the road before you, and nothing around.

Also I recommend closing all browser tabs which you don't need right now. Probably that's the most difficult for myself. Sometimes I find dozens of open tabs in my browser. I was very happy when the "Groups" feature was released in Chrome, which I use on my work laptop. "Great idea" I thought. "Now I can organize all these tabs". But a couple weeks later I understood that the situation became even worse. Now I had not only dozens of tabs, but dozens of "groups", and each of them had many tabs inside. **When we try to organize the chaos, we get just an organized chaos.**   

Another point for optimization is time we spend for configuration of a new laptop. If something happens with your laptop it's a disaster. You must restore all apps you need; restore all configurations: OS, IDE, browser plug-ins, etc. That's a lot of time. But the solution is very simple again. You already know it and use it at your work.  

> Always make backups.

I don't think it's needed to explain why backups matter to people of our profession. But I argue that backups are needed not only for production we are responsible for, but also for our working machines. Weekly backup excludes many problems in case of laptop restore. All you need is a cron-job which gathers all important files into one archive, and any **trusted storage** where you can store the archive.  

What is important to backup. (1) Dotfiles. You can gather and optimize some of your config-files (such as `.vimrc`, `.zshrc`, `.tmux.conf`, etc.) for decades, and it's very sad to lose some of them. In my case I just have a private repo with all these files, and when I need to restore them, I clone the repo and restore all configs using `stow` utility. (2) List of installed apps. It allows you to quickly restore all apps just passing this list to your packet manager. (3) Notes. Since I store all my notes locally in `Markdown` files, this is another critical point for me. Losing the notes means losing my knowledge base and the progress for some tasks and projects. (4) SSH and GPG keys. (5) Your password store.  
I would like to emphasize that the storage on which you keep your backup must be **trusted**, **safe**, and **not be accessible to third parties**.

What also may be useful to backup: browser's plug-ins and history; IDE's configuration and plug-ins. A possibility to restore all these files can significantly reduce idle time and stress level in case of laptop restoration.  

As I said before, probably these recommendations must be the entry point into **What Everyone Knows** methodology. They are easy to apply, and already return a noticeable result. Let's summarize what we've learned so far:

> ### Workspace (hardware) Requirements Memo:
> - Arrange your desk so that you see less of what's going on around you.
> - Remove all objects that might distract you from your field of vision.

> ### Workspace (software) Requirements Memo:
> - Workspace maintenance must be reduced to a minimum.
> - No IMs (Instant Messengers) in the field of view during the work if you work not in them.
> - Only one app on screen at time. (Except the cases when the apps are parts of the same "flow").
> - Always do the local host backups.
