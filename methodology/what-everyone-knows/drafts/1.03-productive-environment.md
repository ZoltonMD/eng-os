# The Hardware: Distraction-Free Environment
Talking about deep work, one more thing I'd like to point out is the productive environment, your work place and things that surround you. *You'll never be able to concentrate if your workspace is in the way of your family members' path to the fridge.* And this is **What Everyone Knows.** 

Bellow in this *(sub-)* chapter I'll give you few **recommendations** which can be easily integrated into your workflow and work environment. But these are not a strict rules (like all I tell about in this project), these are just a hints and tricks which can help you to reduce the environmental pressure to your brain, and increase your concentration ability, to *work less but do more*.
Some of these recommendations can be applicable only to your home workplace, others - only to an office. Don't worry if you can't apply all of them, this is absolutely normal. But remember, the more (of them) you can apply, the greater (their) positive impact will be.  

First, talking about the surroundings, I’d like to talk about your work desk and its placement in the room. After yet another move, while arranging the furniture in a new apartment, I made a very serious mistake that I soon regretted. I didn't have a separate office, so I set up my desk in the living room in such a way that, while sitting at it, I could see the entire room. So, working at the desk I saw all that was going on in the living room. Before I did it, I didn't assume how distractive and destructive this decision would be, even if there is no one in the room except yourself. When your eyes see the entire room, your brain latches onto every detail and each thing that's out of place. The cup you have forgotten to remove from the coffee table, the houseplant that you mustn't forget to water, the dust you noticed on the picture frame, and so on. My brain was overloaded with all this information which it continuously got from the environment. When there is someone in the room, the number of distractions multiplies, because your restless brain is trying to control all movements and changes in the room which it can see from its point of view.  

Once I turned my desk so that I was facing the wall, the situation improved. It became much easier to focus because the visual noise got to a minimum. So, let's formulate the first recommendation:  

> The less you see around you, sitting at the desk, the less distractions you get from the environment.  

Sounds like something very obvious, but I see this mistake very often in people's homes and offices. (This is one of these recommendations which may be beyond your control sometimes, in office spaces, for example.)  
The sitting that way that you see the door can be useful for people who work with clients, but for "knowledge-workers" (I classify engineers specifically into this category) that can be very distractive, and lower your productivity to almost zero.  

Another point the first recommendation can be applied to is "stuff" at your desk. Very often people have many small things at their desks: sticking notes, photos, Funko Pop collections, and many other things that are dear to them. I understand why people collect these items on their desks, but from a productiveness' point of view, all these items are subject to the same rule: "the more items before your eyes - the more distractions your brain gets". Every time you look at your partner photo your brain switches to thoughts about your partner, every time when you see your Funky Pop collection you think which one you must buy next. Every eye movement creates another context switch, and your brain just can't ignore these interruptions, because for it those are just another batch of information it got through the input device (eyes) and it has to process this data.

> The number of objects you see while working is directly proportional to the number of distractions your brain receives.
